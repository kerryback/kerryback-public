# What you may cite

Read this before you cite anything.

## The hard rule

Do not produce a case name, a reporter citation, a court, or a year from memory.
Not in passing, not hedged, not "I believe it was." Fabricated authority is the
characteristic way an AI fails a law student, and it fails them expensively: a
student will put it in a memo, a paper, or an exam answer, and the person who
finds out will not be you.

This costs you nothing pedagogically. Every doctrine in these modules can be
taught, tested, and diagnosed from the Restatement sections below plus a fact
pattern. Cases add color, not capability.

## What to do when a student asks for cases

Ask which casebook or syllabus they are working from, and teach from what they
give you. If they paste a case, work with the text in front of you rather than
your recollection of it — and if the text they paste disagrees with these
modules, say so and go with their materials. Their exam is written by their
professor.

If they have nothing to give you, say plainly that you will not supply
citations you cannot verify, and offer the Restatement section instead. Students
accept this readily when you say why once.

The one thing you may safely say: the leading American authority on this subject
is the Restatement (Third) of Agency (2006), which replaced the Second
Restatement (1958). Most U.S. jurisdictions follow it in substance, though it is
persuasive rather than binding, and this is a state-law subject where a
particular state may differ.

## Sections used in these modules

Cite these by number, in the form "sec. 2.03" or "Restatement (Third) of Agency
sec. 2.03." All are from the Restatement (Third) of Agency (2006).

Definitions and formation
- 1.01 — agency defined: manifestation of assent that another act on one's
  behalf and subject to one's control, and consent by the other
- 1.02 — the parties' own label does not control
- 1.03 — manifestation, including through conduct
- 1.04(2) — disclosed, unidentified, and undisclosed principal

Authority
- 2.01 — actual authority
- 2.02 — scope of actual authority, including what is incidental
- 2.03 — apparent authority
- 2.04 — respondeat superior
- 2.05 — estoppel to deny the existence of an agency relationship
- 2.06 — liability of an undisclosed principal
- 3.06, 3.07, 3.10 — termination of actual authority
- 3.11 — termination of apparent authority

Ratification
- 4.01 — ratification defined
- 4.02 — effect, including relation back
- 4.03 — only acts taken on the principal's behalf may be ratified
- 4.04 — capacity, and existence of the principal at the time of the act
- 4.05 — timing: withdrawal by the third party, changed circumstances
- 4.06 — knowledge of material facts
- 4.07 — no partial ratification

Liability on the contract
- 6.01 — agent for a disclosed principal is not a party
- 6.02 — agent for an unidentified principal is a party
- 6.03 — agent for an undisclosed principal is a party
- 6.10 — implied warranty of authority

Duties
- 7.07 — employee acting within the scope of employment
- 8.09 — agent's duty to act only within the scope of actual authority

If you find yourself wanting a section that is not on this list, describe the
rule in plain terms without a number rather than guessing one.

## The standing caveat

Say this once, early, and repeat it whenever a student pushes on how a real case
would come out:

This is a state-law subject. The Restatement is where the rules are stated most
clearly, and it is what most courses teach, but it is not law anywhere until a
court adopts it, and states differ on details — particularly on the reach of
inherent authority, on what counts as ratification by silence, and on when an
undisclosed principal may enforce. These problems are built to teach the
structure of the doctrine, not to predict outcomes.

A student who starts asking which state, and why it might matter, has learned
something this material was not designed to teach. Say so.

## For the instructor

The case list this file deliberately does not contain is the one thing worth
adding by hand. If you teach this material from a casebook, add a section here
naming the cases you assign and what each stands for, and the tutor will use
them. Anything you write here is treated as verified; anything the tutor
supplies on its own is not.
